# Update - 10 September 2026

## Headline result

Today was spent attacking one objection to the 8 September validation report:
*maybe the Planet Nine fit fails only because the priors were too narrow.*

Two experiments were run, in opposite directions — one tightening the prior on
the semi-major axis, one opening it as wide as physics allows. The answer is
the same both ways, and it is now much stronger than before:

> **Over the widest defensible parameter box — `a` ∈ (0, 50000] AU,
> `e` ∈ [0, 0.99] — the best fit achievable by any orbit that stays more than
> 30 AU from Earth is RMS = 717.4 arcsec = 143.5σ. Exact (RMS ≈ 0) solutions
> do exist, but there are only five of them and every one places the object
> 0.004-0.017 AU from Earth on a short-period orbit that completes a whole
> number of revolutions between the detections.**

The 717 arcsec misfit is therefore **not** an artefact of the prior, the
starting point, or the sampler. It is a property of the data.

A second, methodological result came out of the tightening experiment and is
arguably just as important for how this project reports its numbers:

> **Narrowing the `a` prior to 2000 AU takes P9's worst split-Rhat from 3.35 to
> 1.06 and its acceptance from 0.24 to 0.46 — while the residual stays at
> ~725 arcsec. Convergence diagnostics can be manufactured by tightening a
> prior. Rhat must never be quoted without prior-edge occupancy beside it.**

### 1.3 What experiment A means

* Rhat improves for every single target, in one case by a factor of three, but
  **the number of converged parameter-target pairs stays at 1 of 25** —
  identical to the reference runs. The sampler is not converging; it is being
  confined.
* Coverage rises 4/25 → 9/25, still nowhere near the 24/25 a correct 95%
  interval should deliver. And the rise is not skill: Sedna's `a` interval is
  now [1 744, 2 000] AU against a true 548 AU — wrong by a factor of three,
  with 39.6% of its samples jammed on the wall.
* Leleākūhonua, whose true `a` = 1 085 AU sits closest to the ceiling, gets
  *worse* coverage (4/5 → 2/5). A ceiling that happens to land near the right
  answer is a coincidence, not a method.
* Uranus and Neptune, whose true `a` is nowhere near 2 000 AU and whose
  posteriors never touch either edge, barely move. Their failure was never
  about the ceiling.

**Conclusion of A:** tightening `A_MAX` improves the convergence diagnostics
without improving the fit, and on objects where truth is known it does not
improve — and can degrade — agreement with that truth. It is prior-tightening
masquerading as convergence.

## Part 2 - Experiment B: the widest box, `a` ∈ (0, 50000] AU, `e` ∈ [0, 0.99]

The complementary question. `a = 0` is a zero-period singularity, so the floor
was set to 0.1 AU — small enough to be irrelevant, corresponding to a 12-day
orbit.

### 2.1 The least-squares global search

7 260 bounded least-squares fits: 4 000 from random points across the box, plus
3 260 seeded on the **aliasing resonances** — values of `a` whose period divides
one of the observation gaps. On such an orbit the object completes a whole
number of revolutions between two epochs and returns to the same sky position,
so the model can match those detections exactly regardless of physics. Seeding
them deliberately matters: a purely random multi-start search finds them only
by luck.

**Exact solutions do exist.** Seven of 7 260 starts converged below 1 arcsec,
forming five distinct orbits (the sixth and seventh are the ±i mirror pair of
the last one):

**Table 4 — Every RMS ≈ 0 solution in the box** (`wide_prior_global_wide.csv`)

| `a` (AU) | `e` | i (deg) | q (AU) | Q (AU) | Period (yr) | **Revolutions across the 24 yr arc** | **Closest approach to Earth** | Apparent sky motion there |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0932 | 0.1088 | 19.22 | 0.974 | 1.212 | 1.143 | **21.00** | 0.0153 AU (2.3 M km) | 1 004 arcsec/hr |
| 1.1685 | 0.3850 | 160.78 | 0.719 | 1.618 | 1.263 | **19.00** | 0.0172 AU (2.6 M km) | 3 327 arcsec/hr |
| 1.3680 | 0.5526 | 160.77 | 0.612 | 2.124 | 1.600 | **15.00** | 0.0173 AU (2.6 M km) | 4 774 arcsec/hr |
| 1.6822 | 0.6864 | −160.77 | 0.527 | 2.837 | 2.182 | **11.00** | 0.0173 AU (2.6 M km) | 6 023 arcsec/hr |
| 8.3200 | 0.8793 | ±19.23 | 1.005 | 15.635 | 23.998 | **1.00** | 0.0041 AU (0.6 M km) | 2 279 arcsec/hr |

All five fit to between 4×10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁸ arcsec — exact to machine precision.
And all five are the same artefact, in three independent ways:

1. **The revolution count is an integer in every case** (21, 19, 15, 11, 1).
   These are pure aliasing solutions.
2. **All five sit essentially on top of the observer**, 0.004-0.017 AU, i.e.
   1.6 to 6.7 lunar distances. At zero separation the observer-to-object
   direction is undefined, so *any* sky position can be matched exactly. This
   is a coordinate singularity of the residual, not a fit.
3. **All five are inner-solar-system orbits** with q = 0.53-1.00 AU and
   Q = 1.2-15.6 AU, sweeping the sky at 1 000-6 000 arcsec/hour. IRAS and AKARI
   would have recorded a streak across many detector rows, not a point source,
   and an object 2 million km away at 1 AU from the Sun would have been an
   overwhelming infrared source.

### 2.2 Nothing exists in between

**Table 5 — The residual landscape is strictly bimodal**
(`wide_prior_global_wide.csv`, `wide_prior_profile_wide.csv`)

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Converged solutions | 7 260 |
| With RMS < 1 arcsec | 7 (all near-Earth aliases, `a` = 1.09-8.32 AU) |
| **With 1 arcsec < RMS < 700 arcsec** | **2** |
| With closest approach ≥ 30 AU | 2 402 |
| Best RMS among those | **717.489 arcsec = 143.5σ** (`a` = 25 274 AU, `e` = 0.9888, r = 282.8 AU) |
| 5th percentile of their RMS | 724.9 arcsec |
| Profile minimum over all `a` ≥ 200 AU | **717.433 arcsec** at `a` = 27 332 AU |
| Profile minimum over all `a` < 200 AU | 839.7 arcsec |
| Profile value at `a` = 50 000 AU | 1 977.7 arcsec |

There is a five-order-of-magnitude empty gap between the unphysical exact
solutions and the 717 arcsec floor that every genuinely distant orbit hits.

The profile also shows why pushing `a` beyond ~28 000 AU is counter-productive:
with `e` capped at 0.99 the perihelion obeys q = a(1−e) ≥ a/100, so past
a ≈ 28 200 AU the orbit can no longer reach in to the ~282 AU the data demand,
and the residual climbs back to 1 978 arcsec at the top of the box. The minimum
is genuinely interior, not pinned to an edge.

**Figure:** `wide_prior_profile_wide.png` — upper panel, RMS versus `a` with the
five exact solutions marked as stars; lower panel, closest approach versus `a`,
where those same five sit four orders of magnitude below everything else.

Three things follow.

1. **Widening the box does not find a better orbit.** The best sample among
   480 000 draws still misses by 717.6 arcsec — the same 144σ, with the same
   ±1 200 arcsec opposite-sign AKARI right-ascension signature. It lands on
   exactly the floor the least-squares search independently proves is the
   global optimum for a distant object.
2. **The pile-up moves rather than disappears.** With `a` free to 50 000 AU the
   `a` posterior finally leaves both edges (0.0% at each) — and 23.9% of `e`
   samples immediately take up residence against the 0.99 wall. The posterior
   presses against whichever bound is nearest, because the likelihood keeps
   rewarding higher eccentricity right up to the limit.
3. **The sampler never finds the exact solutions at all.** Least squares locates
   five machine-precision fits inside this box; emcee, started from a dispersed
   prior, finishes at RMS 2 955 arcsec with 25 of 32 walkers stuck and
   Rhat = 222. The MCMC is not merely imprecise here — it fails to locate
   optima that a multi-start optimiser finds routinely.

### 2.5 What experiment B settles

Asked directly — *is there any solution with RMS ≈ 0?* — the answer is:

> **Yes, exactly five. Every one places the object 0.004-0.017 AU from Earth on
> a 1-24 year inner-solar-system orbit completing a whole number of revolutions
> between the detections. Restricted to orbits that are actually distant, the
> best achievable fit anywhere in `a` ∈ (0, 50000] AU, `e` ∈ [0, 0.99] is
> RMS = 717.4 arcsec = 143.5σ.**

Note in passing that the best distant solution sits at r = 282.8 AU, just
outside the 259.9 AU escape-velocity ceiling computed from the *observed*
proper motion — which is consistent, since that solution does not reproduce the
observed motion in the first place.

## Part 3 - Code changes

### 3.1 `run_orbit-mcmc.py` — prior bounds exposed on the command line

```python
parser.add_argument("--a-max", type=float, default=A_MAX)
parser.add_argument("--a-min", type=float, default=A_MIN)
parser.add_argument("--e-max", type=float, default=E_MAX)
```

with validation (`--a-min` must be positive, `--a-max` must exceed it, `--e-max`
must lie in (0, 1) since e ≥ 1 is unbound) and a `global A_MIN, A_MAX, E_MAX`
assignment in `main()`. Every consumer — `log_prior`, `build_initial_positions`,
the boundary-contact diagnostic and the header line — already read those
globals, so nothing else had to change.

**Why we need it.** Prior sensitivity is the first question anyone should ask
of a posterior pressed against a wall. Before today, answering it meant editing
the source, which is exactly the kind of change that does not get recorded in
the output file. Now the prior appears in the diagnostics header of every run.

### 3.2 `validation/run_mcmc_validation.py` — matching `--a-max`, richer CSV

Adds `--a-max` (overriding the per-target `100·r` rule) and writes four new
columns into every result row — `a_min_prior`, `a_max_prior`, `acceptance`,
`frac_upper`, `frac_lower` — so a results CSV is now self-describing and two
runs can be compared without going back to the logs.

### 3.3 New: `search_wide_prior.py`

A dedicated global search answering "does *any* orbit fit?", in three parts:

1. `global_search()` — bounded least squares from random starts across the box,
   optionally seeded with extra `a` values.
2. `commensurate_a()` — enumerates the aliasing resonances, `a = (gap/n)^(2/3)`
   for each of the three observation gaps and n = 1…400, giving 815 seed values
   between 0.10 and 8.32 AU.
3. `profile_over_a()` — best achievable RMS at each of many fixed `a`, with
   warm-starting along the grid, which is what localises *where* any solution
   lives.
4. `monte_carlo()` — the same free fit applied to noisy replicas.

Outputs three CSVs and the two-panel figure.

## Part 4 - A methodological trap worth recording

The first version of `search_wide_prior.py` imposed the box with a **sigmoid
change of variables** (`a` squashed in log₁₀, `e` squashed into [0, 0.99]),
which is the idiom already used in `run_orbit-free_params.py` and is normally
the right way to bound a Levenberg-Marquardt fit.

**It reported that no exact solution exists.** All 7 260 starts converged to the
717 arcsec floor, and the "verdict" block said so in as many words.

That was wrong, and it took a deliberate cross-check against
`run_validation.fit_elements` — which had previously found an exact solution at
a = 8.32 AU — to catch it. Two separate causes:

* The exact roots sit at a **singularity of the residual** (the object is
  0.004 AU from the observer), so the path into them is narrow, and the
  nonlinear transform distorts it enough that LM never threads it.
* Squashing the inclination into [0, π] made things worse. The a = 8.32 AU
  solution has **i = −19.2°**; reaching its positive-i twin requires Ω and ω to
  jump by 180°, which no local optimiser will do.

The fix was to drop the transform and use the raw variables with a flat penalty
outside the box — precisely what `fit_elements` does — and to leave the four
angles entirely unconstrained.

**The lesson for this project:** a negative result from a global search is only
as good as the search's demonstrated ability to find a known positive. Any
future search over this box should be validated against Table 4 before its
"no solution exists" output is believed. This is now recorded in the caveats
section of the validation report.

## Part 5 - Environment: a correction to the 3 September note

The 3 September update recorded that `scipy.optimize.least_squares` crashes the
interpreter in `astro_env` (exit `0xC06D007F`, no traceback), and
`solve_herget.py` was given a hand-written damped Newton solver to work around
it.

That diagnosis was right about the symptom but too broad about the cause.
Measured today:

```
method='lm'   ->  OK, returns [1. 2.]
method='trf'  ->  process dies, exit 0xC06D007F   (this is the DEFAULT)
```

The fault is narrower and now localised exactly:

```
Windows fatal exception: code 0xc06d007f
  File "scipy/linalg/lapack.py", line 1027 in _compute_lwork
  File "scipy/linalg/_decomp_svd.py", line 158 in svd
  File "scipy/optimize/_lsq/trf.py", line 301 in trf_bounds
```

**`scipy.linalg.svd` is what crashes.** `numpy.linalg.svd` on the same matrix is
fine. Because `trf` and `dogbox` call it internally, every *bounded*
least-squares method is unusable — and `trf` is the default, which is why the
trivial two-parameter example in the September 3 note died. `method='lm'`
(MINPACK, no SVD) works perfectly, which is why `run_validation.fit_elements`
has been running all along.

Two practical consequences:

* The hand-written Newton solver in `solve_herget.py` was **not necessary** —
  `method='lm'` would have done. Worth revisiting if that code is ever touched
  again.
* Bounds must be imposed by penalty or transform rather than by
  `bounds=(lo, hi)`. This is the constraint that shaped `search_wide_prior.py`.

Reinstalling `scipy` in `astro_env` is still recommended; it would remove a
real constraint on how any future fitting code can be written.

## Part 7 - Conclusion

1. **The prior is not the problem.** Tightening `a` to 2 000 AU and opening it
   to 50 000 AU both leave the P9 residual at 717-726 arcsec ≈ 145σ. Across
   the three runs that reach the 717 arcsec basin, the fitted ecliptic
   orientation is stable to i = 41.21-41.27° and Ω = 129.148-129.162°
   (ω is looser, 344.1-347.7°), while `a` and `e` move by whatever the prior
   permits. The orientation is the only thing the data actually pin down.
2. **Exact solutions exist but are physically impossible.** Five of them, all
   aliasing orbits 1.6-6.7 lunar distances from Earth moving at
   1 000-6 000 arcsec/hour. Their existence is a property of a 6-parameter
   model fitted to 6 observables (dof = 0), not evidence for anything.
3. **Excluding those, the global floor is 717.4 arcsec = 143.5σ**, established
   by 7 260 independent least-squares fits, a 240-point profile, 60 Monte Carlo
   replicas and two MCMC chains — all agreeing to within 0.3%.
4. **Convergence diagnostics are not evidence of a good fit.** The single most
   "converged-looking" run of the day (Rhat = 1.06, acceptance 0.46, zero stuck
   walkers) was also one of the worst fits, and its precision came entirely
   from a prior wall. Rhat, prior-edge occupancy and the actual residual must
   be reported together.
5. **The conclusion of 3 and 8 September stands and is now much harder to
   argue with:** the IRAS and two AKARI positions are not consistent with a
   single bound two-body orbit at the assumed 5 arcsec uncertainty.

### Suggested next steps

* **A fourth detection remains the only decisive fix.** It moves dof from 0 to
  2, makes χ² a real goodness-of-fit statistic, kills the aliasing family
  outright (a whole number of revolutions between *three* independent gaps is
  vastly more constrained), and collapses the `a`-`e` valley.
* **Re-examine the AKARI_2 right ascension**, per Part 5 of the 3 September
  update. Today's work adds weight: no orbit anywhere in a six-decade box can
  absorb a +1 200 / −1 300 arcsec opposite-sign pair in RA while leaving
  declination consistent at ~200 AU.
* **Repair `scipy`** in `astro_env` so bounded optimisation becomes available.
* **Report the profile likelihood in `a`**, not an MCMC posterior, for any
  number that has to be quoted from the existing three detections.
